\section{Introduction}
In this assignment, I was tasked with solving two polynomial regression problems based on an expansion project for a geothermal power plant:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Phase 1 (Steam Turbine Optimization - var1):} Predicting the Net Power Score ($y$) from 6 turbine control settings ($x_1$ to $x_6$), representing percentage deviations in valve adjustments, coolant flow, pump pressure, and blade pitch.
    \item \textbf{Phase 2 (Subterranean Thermal Reservoir Mapping - var2):} Predicting the Thermal Anomaly Score ($y$) at various 3D spatial coordinates ($x_1, x_2, x_3$) around the basecamp to locate good sites for drilling geothermal wells.
\end{itemize}

My goal for both problems was to find the right polynomial degree and regularization method so that the models capture the underlying physical curves without underfitting or overfitting. I evaluated every model using 5-fold cross-validation on the training set, comparing Mean Squared Error (MSE) and $R^2$ scores.

\section{Phase 1: Steam Turbine Optimization (var1)}

\subsection{Data Exploration and Baseline}
I started by inspecting the dataset files \texttt{BT2024032\_train\_var1.csv} and \texttt{BT2024032\_test\_var1.csv}. Both have 1,000 samples with no missing values. The six input features are bounded in $[-1.0, 1.0]$. The target variable $y$ has a mean of $0.74$, a standard deviation of $3.13$, and ranges between $-8.83$ and $+11.50$. 

When I checked the linear correlation between individual inputs and $y$, the numbers were quite low (the strongest was around $-0.26$ with $x_5$). This showed that a simple linear equation would not work well because turbine efficiency depends on joint interactions between valves and pressures.

To confirm this, I trained a baseline linear regression model (Degree 1). Using 5-fold cross-validation, it gave a validation MSE of $8.5302$ and an $R^2$ of only $0.1248$. Capturing only 12.5\% of the variance showed clear underfitting (high bias).

\subsection{Testing Different Degrees and Regularization}
Next, I generated polynomial features from degree 1 up to degree 6. With 6 variables, the number of terms grows quickly: degree 2 has 27 terms, degree 3 has 83 terms, degree 4 has 209 terms, degree 5 has 461 terms, and degree 6 has 923 terms.

I tested standard Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) alongside Ridge (L2 penalty) and Lasso (L1 penalty) using 5-fold cross-validation. Table~\ref{tab:var1} summarizes the results.

\subsection{Why I Chose Degree 5 with Lasso}
Looking at Table~\ref{tab:var1} and Figure~\ref{fig:var1_curve}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Standard OLS works fine up to degree 4, but starts overfitting heavily at degree 5. The validation MSE jumps from $0.752$ to $1.284$, and at degree 6 it shoots up to $3.810$. Because each fold has only 800 training samples, estimating 461 to 923 unconstrained parameters leads to high variance.
    \item Lasso (L1 regularization) solved this problem cleanly. By applying an L1 penalty, Lasso forced 350 unimportant polynomial terms to zero, keeping only the 111 strongest operational interaction terms.
    \item This reduced the validation MSE to \textbf{0.3151} and achieved an $R^2$ score of \textbf{96.71\%}.
    \item When I pushed to Degree 6, the validation error stopped improving and rose slightly to $0.3203$. This showed that degree 5 is the sweet spot.
\end{itemize}

\section{Phase 2: Subterranean Thermal Reservoir Mapping (var2)}

\subsection{Data Exploration and Baseline}
For Phase 2, the inputs are 3 spatial coordinate offsets in meters ($x_1$ = East-West, $x_2$ = North-South, $x_3$ = Depth). The target $y$ is the Thermal Anomaly Score. All coordinate values in train and test are within $[-1.0, 1.0]$. The target variable has a mean of $2.38$, a standard deviation of $7.22$, and ranges from $-30.10$ to $+39.87$.

I noticed that $x_2$ had a moderate linear correlation of $0.50$ with $y$, while $x_1$ and $x_3$ had almost zero linear correlation. The baseline linear model (Degree 1) had a validation MSE of $38.88$ and an $R^2$ of only $0.243$, which is severe underfitting. Underground thermal plumes curve sharply in 3D space, so a much higher degree polynomial is required.

\subsection{Degree Exploration from 1 to 16}
Because there are only 3 variables, the number of polynomial terms grows more slowly than in Phase 1 ($\binom{d+3}{3}$ terms). This allowed me to test degrees from 1 up to 16 using 5-fold cross-validation. Table~\ref{tab:var2} shows how the models behaved.

\subsection{Why I Chose Degree 12 with Ridge}
Looking at Table~\ref{tab:var2} and Figure~\ref{fig:var2_curve}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unregularized OLS reaches its minimum error at Degree 8 (MSE = $0.2788$), but begins overfitting at degree 9 (MSE = $0.2939$) and degree 10 (MSE = $0.3428$).
    \item Ridge regression (L2 penalty) prevents this overfitting by penalizing large weights. As the degree increases, Ridge automatically raises the regularization strength $\alpha$ (from $0.07$ at degree 8 up to $1.21$ at degree 12).
    \item At \textbf{Degree 12} (454 features), Ridge reaches the lowest validation MSE across all experiments: \textbf{0.2579}, explaining \textbf{99.51\%} of the variance.
    \item Beyond degree 12, the validation error starts creeping upward ($0.2615$ at degree 14, $0.2658$ at degree 16). This confirmed that degree 12 is the optimal choice.
\end{itemize}

I also checked the predictions for any physical inconsistencies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Residual Normality:} I ran a Jarque-Bera test on the residuals, getting a $p$-value of $0.66$ ($p > 0.05$). This confirms that the residuals are pure Gaussian sensor noise with $\sigma = 0.51$.
    \item \textbf{Spatial Bias:} The correlations between residuals and spatial coordinates ($x_1, x_2, x_3$) were all below $0.015$, confirming there is no directional drift.
    \item \textbf{Test Range:} The test predictions range from $-29.51$ to $+39.18$, which fits the training target range ($-30.10$ to $+39.87$) without boundary spikes.
\end{itemize}


\section{Discussion on Bias, Variance, and Regularization}
Across both problems, finding the right model came down to balancing bias and variance:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High Bias (Underfitting):} Models with degree 1 to 3 were too simple. They couldn't bend enough to follow the actual physical behavior, leaving large errors and low $R^2$ scores in both phases.
    \item \textbf{High Variance (Overfitting):} When using unregularized OLS with high degrees, the model tried to fit every little ripple in the training data. As soon as the number of terms approached the number of training samples, the validation error spiked.
    \item \textbf{Why Different Regularizers Worked Best:} 
    In Phase 1, Lasso (L1) was the clear winner because the underlying turbine relationship is sparse. Out of 461 possible combinations of valves and blade angles, only 111 actually mattered. Lasso dropped the other 350 terms, which prevented overfitting.
    In Phase 2, Ridge (L2) was more suitable because heat diffusion through rock forms continuous, smooth gradients. L2 regularization shrank all 454 coefficients smoothly, keeping the 3D surface stable right up to degree 12.
\end{itemize}
